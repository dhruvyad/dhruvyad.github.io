Visualizing Transformers, from the Dot Product Up

A transformer looks complicated because it is large, not because it is deep. Almost all of it is one operation, repeated: multiply two lists of numbers together and add up the result. This piece builds a picture of the whole thing starting from that single operation, adding one idea at a time and never introducing a second thing before the first is visible.

Most explanations of transformers begin at the block diagram — boxes labelled multi-head attention and feed-forward, arrows between them — and work downward. That order has a problem: the boxes are named after what they are for, not what they do, so the diagram can be memorised without anything being understood.

This goes the other way. We start with the smallest operation in the model, get a picture of it that survives being poked at, and then build every larger structure out of that picture. By the end nothing in a transformer should look like a new idea — only like more of the same idea, arranged differently.

One number from two lists

The dot product takes two equal-length lists of numbers and returns one number. Multiply the first entries together, multiply the second entries together, and so on, then add up everything you made:

a \cdot b = \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i b_i

That is the entire definition. What makes it interesting is what the answer means. It is large and positive when the two lists rise and fall together, near zero when they have no relationship, and negative when one goes up wherever the other goes down. It is a similarity score.

Two things to take from this figure, because everything later depends on them. First, the cost: $n$ multiplies and $n-1$ adds, which people round to $2n$ floating-point operations. Second, the meaning: a dot product is how a computer asks are these two things alike? When a model needs to decide which words to pay attention to, this is the question it asks, and this is the only tool it has for asking it.

A matrix multiply is a grid of dot products

One dot product compares one pair. Usually we want every pair: every row of one table against every column of another. Stack the comparisons into a grid and you have a matrix multiply. There is no new operation here, only bookkeeping — the cell at row $i$, column $j$ of the result is the dot product of row $i$ of the left matrix with column $j$ of the right one.

The cost follows immediately. An $M \times K$ matrix times a $K \times N$ matrix produces $MN$ output cells, each one a dot product of length $K$:

\text{FLOPs} = 2 \cdot M \cdot N \cdot K

Nearly every performance claim about a language model is this formula, applied a few dozen times and added up. It is worth being comfortable with it.

Attention is two matrix multiplies and a softmax

Now the first structure that is specific to transformers — and it introduces no new arithmetic at all.

Give every token three vectors: a query (what am I looking for?), a key (what do I offer?), and a value (what will I contribute if chosen?). To decide how much token $i$ should care about token $j$, compare $i$'s query with $j$'s key — a dot product. Doing that for every pair is a matrix multiply, $QK^\top$.

Those raw scores are then turned into weights that sum to one, with a softmax across each row, and the weights are used to blend the value vectors together. Written out, it is the one line from the original paper:

\text{Attention}(Q,K,V) = \text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d}}\right)V

Three observations, each of which will matter later.

The score panel is square in the number of tokens. Every token compares against every other, so the work grows as $T^2$. Doubling the context quadruples this step. That single fact is behind nearly every difficulty with long context.

The division by $\sqrt{d}$ is not decoration. A dot product of $d$ random terms grows with $d$, and large scores make softmax nearly one-hot, which flattens the gradients that train it. Dividing keeps the scores in a range where softmax stays soft — switch it off in the figure and watch the weights collapse onto a single token.

Only the weights are learned indirectly. Nothing in the attention step has parameters of its own; $Q$, $K$ and $V$ arrive from three ordinary matrix multiplies on the token representations. Attention is a way of using the numbers, not a place where numbers are stored.

Scaling the picture

Everything above draws each number as its own cell. That is the right level for learning an operation and completely wrong for looking at a model: the smallest weight matrix in a frontier model has a few million entries, and there are tens of thousands of matrices. Cells are five orders of magnitude too small a unit.

So above a certain zoom the picture has to encode shape and role rather than values. A weight matrix becomes a rectangle whose proportions are its real dimensions and whose colour is what it does. That alone is not enough — twenty thousand rectangles is a wall, as anyone who has seen a full-model diagram knows.

The thing that makes it fit is that models are enormously repetitive. A 78-layer network is not 78 different things; it is one thing 78 times. A 256-expert layer is one expert 256 times. Fold the repeats into a multiplicity badge and a model with 19,767 weight matrices collapses to six stages and about two dozen distinct shapes — which fits on a screen with room to spare.

Notice what the collapse reveals for free. The MoE layers split into two kinds, ×18 and ×57, because only some of them own a sparse-attention indexer and the rest reuse a neighbour's. That is a real architectural decision, and it shows up here as structure rather than as a footnote.

What comes next

We now have the two pieces every remaining structure is made of. The rest of this series builds upward without introducing another kind of arithmetic:

  • Multiple heads — the same operation run several times in parallel on slices of the representation, so different heads can attend for different reasons.
  • The transformer block — attention and a feed-forward network, each added back into a running residual stream rather than replacing it.
  • The full network — that block, stacked; and what the stack looks like end to end.
  • Mixture of experts — replacing the one feed-forward network with many, and choosing a few per token.

Once that picture exists, the sequel to this piece — Where Tokens Per Second Comes From — takes the same structures and asks what they cost to run.

This is a first draft, and deliberately unfinished. The three figures above establish a visual language: a number is a coloured cell, a vector is a row of them, a matrix is a grid, and red and blue mean positive and negative everywhere. Everything still to come will be built from those same primitives, so the language is worth settling before there is a lot of it.
