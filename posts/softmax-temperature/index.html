Softmax, Slowly

Everyone can write down the softmax formula. Fewer people can say what its temperature parameter is really doing, or why the function is unchanged when you add seven to every input. Both facts fall out of the same observation.

Softmax turns a vector of real numbers into a probability distribution. For logits $z_1, \dots, z_K$ the probability it assigns to class $i$ is

p_i = \frac{e^{z_i}}{\sum_{j=1}^{K} e^{z_j}}.

The name is a little unfortunate. It is not a soft version of the $\max$ function — that would return a number near the largest input. It is a soft version of $\arg\max$: a smoothed one-hot indicator of which input is largest. The function that actually smooths $\max$ is the log-sum-exp, $\operatorname{lse}(z) = \log \sum_j e^{z_j}$, and the two are related: softmax is exactly the gradient of log-sum-exp. If you have ever wondered why those two show up together so often, that is why. It arrived in machine learning via Bridle, who noticed that a network trained with this output layer could be read as estimating posterior class probabilities.

Temperature

The usual generalisation divides the logits by a positive constant $T$ before exponentiating:

p_i(T) = \frac{e^{z_i / T}}{\sum_{j} e^{z_j / T}}.

Because dividing by $T$ scales the gaps between logits, $T$ controls how much a given gap matters. Two logits separated by $1.0$ are separated by $10$ at $T = 0.1$ and by $0.125$ at $T = 8$. Drag the slider and watch the distribution slide between its two limits.

The two limits are worth stating precisely:

  • $T \to 0^+$. The largest logit's share tends to $1$ and everything else tends to $0$ — hard $\arg\max$. Sampling at very low temperature is therefore nearly deterministic.
  • $T \to \infty$. All the scaled logits approach $0$, so every $e^{z_i/T} \to 1$ and $p_i \to 1/K$ — the uniform distribution, which ignores the logits entirely.

This one knob gets reused in a few different places, which is worth noticing because the intent differs each time. In knowledge distillation, a high temperature is applied deliberately to expose the small probabilities a teacher network assigns to wrong classes — the "dark knowledge" that a hard $\arg\max$ throws away. In calibration, a single $T$ is fit on a validation set after training to fix the fact that modern networks are systematically overconfident. In text generation it is a sampling control, where the failure modes at both extremes — degenerate repetition when too low, incoherence when too high — motivated truncated sampling schemes instead.

Shift-invariance

Add the same constant $c$ to every logit and nothing happens. The proof is one line: the factor $e^{c}$ appears in the numerator and in every term of the denominator, so it cancels.

\frac{e^{z_i + c}}{\sum_j e^{z_j + c}} = \frac{e^{c} e^{z_i}}{e^{c} \sum_j e^{z_j}} = \frac{e^{z_i}}{\sum_j e^{z_j}}

So logits are only meaningful up to a shift — softmax reads differences, not values. The figure below computes the probabilities from the shifted logits every frame, so you can watch the invariance hold rather than take my word for it.

This is not just a curiosity; it is the reason softmax can be computed at all. Written naively, $e^{z_i}$ overflows a float64 somewhere around $z_i = 710$. Since we are free to shift, every real implementation subtracts the maximum first:

def softmax(z, T=1.0): z = z / T z = z - z.max() # free, by shift-invariance e = np.exp(z) # largest term is now exp(0) = 1 return e / e.sum()

After the subtraction the largest exponent is exactly $e^0 = 1$, so overflow is impossible. Underflow is still possible for very negative entries, but those round to $0$, which is the right answer anyway. The same trick is why you should reach for a fused log_softmax rather than composing log(softmax(z)). The fused version computes $z_i - \operatorname{lse}(z)$ directly and stays accurate even when $p_i$ is far too small to represent.

The derivative

One more thing worth having memorised, because it shows up in every backward pass. The Jacobian of softmax is

\frac{\partial p_i}{\partial z_j} = p_i (\delta_{ij} - p_j),

where $\delta_{ij}$ is $1$ when $i = j$ and $0$ otherwise. Two consequences read straight off it. The diagonal terms $p_i(1 - p_i)$ vanish whenever $p_i$ is near $0$ or near $1$ — a saturated softmax has almost no gradient, which is exactly the vanishing-gradient story told about sigmoids. And the off-diagonal terms $-p_i p_j$ are negative: probability is conserved, so pushing one class up necessarily pushes the others down.

The saturation problem is also why cross-entropy is the loss of choice rather than, say, squared error on the probabilities. Composing softmax with cross-entropy collapses to a gradient of $p - y$ — the raw prediction error, with the troublesome $p_i(1 - p_i)$ factor cancelled out. The two functions are designed to be used together, and taking their gradients separately throws that away.

What to remember

Softmax reads differences between logits, not logits. Temperature rescales those differences — small $T$ magnifies them toward $\arg\max$, large $T$ erases them toward uniform. The freedom to shift is what makes the computation numerically safe, and the Jacobian $p_i(\delta_{ij} - p_j)$ explains both why saturated outputs stop learning and why cross-entropy is its natural partner.
